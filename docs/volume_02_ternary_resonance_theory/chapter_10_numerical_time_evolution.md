# Numerical Time Evolution

## 1. Purpose

This chapter defines the numerical time-evolution layer of Ternary Resonance Theory.

The numerical layer realizes the coupled architecture:

`continuous dynamics`

`→ resonance evaluation`

`→ ternary target generation`

`→ scheduler evolution`

`→ neutral routing`

`→ committed ternary execution`

`→ feedback`

while preserving the mathematical state semantics defined in the preceding chapters.

The chapter formalizes:

- physical time;
- numerical time;
- execution tacts;
- scheduler coordinates;
- discrete integration;
- phase wrapping;
- retained-frequency updates;
- resonance evaluation;
- target sampling;
- target registration;
- event detection;
- neutral-routing updates;
- committed writeback;
- feedback ordering;
- multirate evolution;
- deterministic replay;
- numerical error;
- exact categorical invariants;
- trace generation;
- numerical validation.

The numerical realization must preserve the canonical balanced ternary execution kernel:

`-1/0/1`.

---

## 2. Numerical State

Let:

`x_num[n]`

denote the complete numerical state at numerical step:

`n`.

A representative numerical state may contain:

`x_num[n] = (x_C[n], x_R[n], t_target[n], t_exec[n], t_pending[n], x_sched[n], x_route[n], x_M[n], x_solver[n])`.

The exact state definition is specialization-specific.

Every result-affecting retained variable belongs to the numerical state or is deterministically reconstructible from it.

---

## 3. Physical Time

Physical time is denoted:

`t`.

A numerical realization may define:

`t_n = t_0 + n Delta t`

for fixed timestep:

`Delta t > 0`.

Adaptive integration may instead use:

`t_(n+1) = t_n + Delta t_n`.

---

## 4. Numerical Step

The integer:

`n`

indexes numerical evolution.

A numerical step is not automatically identical to:

- physical-time unit;
- execution tact;
- scheduler tact;
- target-evaluation step;
- committed ternary event.

These coordinates remain separately typed.

---

## 5. Execution Tact

Let:

`k`

denote the execution tact.

A mapping:

`k = F_k(n)`

may relate numerical steps to execution tacts.

Several numerical steps may occur within one execution tact.

---

## 6. Scheduler Coordinate

Let:

`s`

denote scheduler state or scheduler phase index.

The scheduler may evolve according to:

`x_sched[k+1] = F_sched(x_sched[k])`.

Scheduler evolution remains distinct from numerical integration.

---

## 7. Target-Evaluation Coordinate

Let:

`m`

denote target-evaluation index.

The mapping between:

`m`

and numerical step:

`n`

must be explicit when they differ.

---

## 8. Multiple Time Coordinates

A complete numerical realization may contain:

`physical time t`

`numerical step n`

`target step m`

`execution tact k`

`scheduler state s`.

No one coordinate is substituted silently for another.

---

## 9. Fixed-Step Integration

A fixed-step numerical integrator uses constant:

`Delta t`.

A generic update is:

`x_C[n+1] = Phi_C(x_C[n], Delta t, x_aux[n])`.

---

## 10. Adaptive-Step Integration

An adaptive solver may use:

`Delta t_n`.

Then:

`x_C[n+1] = Phi_C(x_C[n], Delta t_n, x_aux[n])`.

The timestep sequence becomes result-affecting numerical state where deterministic replay requires it.

---

## 11. Continuous Vector Field

For continuous model:

`dx_C/dt = F_C(x_C, t_exec, x_aux)`

the numerical integrator approximates the flow generated by:

`F_C`.

The numerical update is not identical to the continuous vector field.

---

## 12. Explicit Euler Realization

A first-order explicit realization may use:

`x_C[n+1] = x_C[n] + Delta t F_C(x_C[n], t_exec[n], x_aux[n])`.

This is one numerical specialization.

---

## 13. Higher-Order Integrators

A specialization may use:

- Runge-Kutta methods;
- multistep methods;
- symplectic integrators;
- implicit methods;
- operator splitting;
- event-aware hybrid solvers.

The selected integrator belongs to the numerical realization contract.

---

## 14. Integrator Choice

Integrator choice may affect:

- numerical accuracy;
- stability region;
- energy drift;
- event localization;
- deterministic replay;
- computational cost.

It must not alter the formal semantic type of model state.

---

## 15. Phase Numerical State

For oscillator:

`i`

the numerical phase is:

`theta_i[n]`.

The stored representation may use:

`theta_i[n] ∈ [0, 2 pi)`.

---

## 16. Phase Update

A discrete phase update may use:

`theta_i[n+1] = Wrap(theta_i[n] + Delta t F_theta,i[n])`.

---

## 17. Phase Wrapping

The wrapping operator preserves circular state:

`theta_i mod 2 pi`.

A canonical implementation may use a representation equivalent to:

`Wrap(theta) ∈ [0, 2 pi)`.

---

## 18. Phase Wrapping Invariant

For every numerical step:

`theta_i[n]`

must represent a valid point of:

`S^1`.

Adding or subtracting integer multiples of:

`2 pi`

does not change the physical oscillator phase state.

---

## 19. Wrapped versus Unwrapped Phase

The stored wrapped phase and an auxiliary unwrapped phase are distinct representations.

Unwrapped phase may be used for:

- frequency estimation;
- cumulative phase tracking;
- diagnostics.

The underlying oscillator phase remains circular.

---

## 20. Relative Phase Evaluation

Relative phase should use circular semantics:

`Delta theta_ij = Wrap(theta_j - theta_i)`.

Naive subtraction without wrapping may introduce branch-cut artifacts.

---

## 21. Phase Coupling Evaluation

A receiving-state Kuramoto-Sakaguchi contribution may be evaluated as:

`sin(theta_j - theta_i - gamma_effective_i)`.

The numerical implementation must preserve the declared lag sign and receiver index.

---

## 22. Phase Lag

The phase lag remains angular state or parameter.

It is not an explicit temporal delay.

The numerical implementation therefore must not introduce delayed-state semantics unless delay is separately defined.

---

## 23. Retained Frequency State

Let:

`omega_ret_i[n]`

be the retained frequency state.

It may evolve toward:

`omega_target_i[n]`.

---

## 24. Retained Frequency Update

A relaxation specialization may use:

`omega_ret_i[n+1] = omega_ret_i[n] + alpha_i (omega_target_i[n] - omega_ret_i[n])`.

The coefficient:

`alpha_i`

is specialization-specific.

---

## 25. Frequency Target

The target frequency may depend on:

- base frequency;
- executed ternary magnitude;
- switching activity;
- local state;
- control state.

A generic mapping is:

`omega_target_i[n] = F_omega(x_i[n], |t_exec_i[n]|, switch_i[n], ...)`.

---

## 26. Frequency Update Ordering

The numerical realization must define whether retained frequency is updated:

- before phase;
- after phase;
- simultaneously;
- through predictor-corrector iteration.

Different orderings may produce different trajectories.

---

## 27. Memory Preservation

Because retained frequency affects future phase evolution, it belongs to restart-complete numerical state.

---

## 28. Resonance Projection Step

After the required upstream state has been updated, resonance may be evaluated:

`r[n] = P_R(x_C[n], x_aux[n])`.

The evaluation coordinate must be explicit.

---

## 29. Resonance Evaluation Ordering

A realization must define whether:

`P_R`

uses:

- state at `n`;
- state at `n+1`;
- predicted state;
- corrected state.

---

## 30. Resonance Classification Step

The classifier may evaluate:

`k_R[n] = C_R(r[n], x_H[n])`.

The classification remains separately typed from ternary target.

---

## 31. Numerical Resonance Boundary

A resonance boundary may be defined exactly in the mathematical model.

The numerical implementation may evaluate proximity using finite precision.

---

## 32. Boundary Function

For implicit boundary:

`B_R(r) = 0`

the numerical evaluator computes:

`B_R(r[n])`.

---

## 33. Boundary Sign Change

A potential crossing may be detected when:

`B_R(r[n]) B_R(r[n+1]) < 0`.

This detects a sign change under the applicable assumptions.

---

## 34. Tangential Contact

A trajectory may touch:

`B_R = 0`

without a sign change.

Therefore sign-change detection is not sufficient for every contact event.

---

## 35. Event Localization

A crossing may be localized between:

`t_n`

and:

`t_(n+1)`.

Methods may include:

- interpolation;
- subdivision;
- root finding;
- adaptive reduction of timestep.

---

## 36. Exact Event versus Numerical Event

The mathematical crossing time and numerically estimated crossing time are distinct.

Numerical event localization approximates the formal event.

---

## 37. Event Tolerance

A numerical event tolerance may be:

`epsilon_event`.

The value belongs to numerical realization.

It does not redefine the exact mathematical boundary.

---

## 38. Numerical Boundary Band

A numerical boundary band may be:

`|B_R(r)| ≤ epsilon_event`.

This finite band is not identical to:

`∂W_R`.

---

## 39. Boundary Band versus Active Neutral

A numerical boundary band does not automatically mean:

`t_target = 0`

or:

`t_exec = 0`.

Any such mapping must be explicitly defined.

---

## 40. Target Evaluation

The continuous-to-ternary mapping is evaluated as:

`t_target_raw[m] = P_CT(x_source[m], x_aux[m])`.

Its output belongs exactly to:

`{-1, 0, 1}`.

---

## 41. Exact Target State

Once classification completes:

`t_target ∈ {-1, 0, 1}`

exactly.

There is no approximate ternary target.

---

## 42. Numerical Decision Boundary

Floating-point arithmetic may affect which side of a continuous decision boundary an input falls on.

The implementation must define the numerical comparison semantics.

---

## 43. Threshold Comparison

For scalar threshold:

`eta`

a mapping may use:

`z > eta`

`z < -eta`

and the middle region otherwise.

Boundary equality semantics must remain explicit.

---

## 44. Numerical Threshold Tolerance

A specialization may introduce:

`epsilon_target`

for numerical boundary handling.

Its role must be explicitly defined.

---

## 45. Tolerance Does Not Redefine Ternary Domain

Whatever numerical boundary policy is used, the resulting target remains exactly:

`-1`

`0`

or:

`1`.

---

## 46. Target Sampling

If target generation occurs at selected steps:

`m`

the sampled target is:

`t_target_sampled[m]`.

---

## 47. Target Registration

The sampled target may be registered:

`t_target_reg[k]`.

Registration creates retained target state visible to execution control.

---

## 48. Live Target versus Registered Target

A continuously changing upstream classification and a registered execution target may differ.

The implementation must preserve both when both are result-affecting.

---

## 49. Sample-and-Hold

A registered target may remain constant until the next registration event.

This is sample-and-hold behavior.

---

## 50. Target Queue

A realization may queue multiple target requests.

Queue state is result-affecting if request order changes future execution.

---

## 51. Target Queue versus Pending Route

The target queue belongs to upstream request handling.

Pending destination belongs to neutral routing.

They remain distinct.

---

## 52. Scheduler Update

The scheduler state may be updated before or after target registration according to the declared order.

A generic update is:

`x_sched[k+1] = F_sched(x_sched[k], x_ctrl[k])`.

---

## 53. Scheduler Opportunity

A scheduler state may indicate whether an execution operation is eligible.

This eligibility does not guarantee commit.

---

## 54. Scheduler Mode Separation

Scheduler labels remain distinct from:

`-1/0/1`.

A scheduler state called:

`neutralize`

does not equal ternary state:

`0`.

---

## 55. Execution Request Evaluation

At an eligible execution opportunity, the execution layer evaluates:

- current target;
- current executed state;
- pending route;
- route-control state;
- capacity;
- authorization.

---

## 56. Same-State Request

If:

`t_target = t_exec`

the state may remain unchanged.

No opposite route is created.

---

## 57. Neutralization Request

If:

`t_target = 0`

and:

`t_exec = -1`

the valid state-changing edge is:

`-1 → 0`.

If:

`t_exec = 1`

the valid edge is:

`1 → 0`.

---

## 58. Polarization Request from Neutral

If:

`t_exec = 0`

and:

`t_target = 1`

the valid edge is:

`0 → 1`.

If:

`t_target = -1`

the valid edge is:

`0 → -1`.

---

## 59. Opposite Target Detection

An opposite route is required when:

`t_exec = -1`

and:

`t_target = 1`

or:

`t_exec = 1`

and:

`t_target = -1`.

---

## 60. First-Leg Numerical Update

For opposite target:

`-1 → 1`

the first execution update commits:

`t_exec_next = 0`

and registers:

`t_pending_next = 1`.

---

## 61. Reverse First-Leg Numerical Update

For:

`1 → -1`

the first update commits:

`t_exec_next = 0`

and:

`t_pending_next = -1`.

---

## 62. First-Leg Writeback

The first-leg executed state and pending destination should become visible together at the declared commit boundary.

This prevents transient state ambiguity.

---

## 63. Neutral Residence Update

While:

`t_exec = 0`

and:

`t_pending ≠ NONE`

the router may retain:

`t_exec_next = 0`

`t_pending_next = t_pending`.

---

## 64. Neutral Residence Counter

A numerical realization may update:

`n_neutral[k+1] = n_neutral[k] + 1`

during retained neutral routing.

---

## 65. Minimum Residence Test

Second-leg eligibility may require:

`n_neutral ≥ n_min`.

The value:

`n_min`

is specialization-specific.

---

## 66. Second-Leg Numerical Update

If second-leg authorization is granted:

`t_exec = 0`

`t_pending = 1`

produces:

`t_exec_next = 1`

`t_pending_next = NONE`.

---

## 67. Reverse Second Leg

Likewise:

`t_exec = 0`

`t_pending = -1`

produces:

`t_exec_next = -1`

`t_pending_next = NONE`.

---

## 68. Distinct Commit Events

The first and second legs must remain distinct committed state-changing events.

They cannot be numerically compressed into one direct opposite committed write.

---

## 69. Direct Opposite Numerical Prohibition

The numerical implementation must reject or prevent:

`t_exec[n] = -1`

`t_exec[n+1] = 1`.

The reverse case is likewise forbidden.

---

## 70. Exact Transition Invariant

The direct-opposite prohibition is exact.

No numerical tolerance permits:

`-1 → 1`

or:

`1 → -1`.

---

## 71. Route Cancellation Update

A cancellation may clear:

`t_pending`

while retaining:

`t_exec = 0`.

The exact target handling must follow the selected route policy.

---

## 72. Route Replacement Update

A replacement may modify:

`t_pending`

while:

`t_exec = 0`.

This does not constitute a committed polarity transition.

---

## 73. Route Conflict Resolution

If live target and pending destination disagree, the conflict resolver must execute before second-leg writeback.

---

## 74. Capacity Evaluation

A capacity guard may evaluate:

`G_capacity(x_num) ∈ {ALLOW, BLOCK}`.

Capacity blocking does not make a structurally valid edge invalid.

It prevents that commit at the current opportunity.

---

## 75. Blocked First Leg

If first-leg execution is blocked, the current polarized state remains retained under the selected retain policy.

---

## 76. Blocked Second Leg

If second-leg execution is blocked, the system may remain:

`t_exec = 0`

with pending destination preserved.

---

## 77. Commit Writeback

The numerical implementation must define one retained-state writeback boundary.

At that boundary the next committed:

- executed state;
- pending state;
- route state;
- counters

become current state.

---

## 78. Pre-Commit State

State used for evaluation before commit is:

`x^-`.

---

## 79. Post-Commit State

State after commit is:

`x^+`.

The distinction is required when event ordering affects downstream computations.

---

## 80. Feedback Update

After commit, feedback may be computed from the committed executed state:

`u_FB[n+1] = F_FB(t_exec[n+1], x_R[n+1], x_C[n+1])`.

The exact ordering is specialization-specific.

---

## 81. Pre-Commit Feedback Alternative

A different realization may evaluate feedback from:

`t_exec[n]`.

The selected convention must be explicit.

---

## 82. Feedback Ordering

The numerical contract must specify whether feedback uses:

- previous committed state;
- newly committed state;
- predicted state;
- averaged state.

---

## 83. Coupled Step Ordering

A concrete numerical realization must define the complete step sequence.

A representative sequence is:

`continuous update`

`→ retained-frequency update`

`→ phase wrapping`

`→ resonance evaluation`

`→ synchronization/coherence evaluation`

`→ target generation`

`→ target registration`

`→ scheduler update`

`→ route evaluation`

`→ authorization`

`→ ternary commit`

`→ feedback evaluation`.

---

## 84. Ordering Is Part of the Numerical Model

If changing this sequence changes results, the ordering is result-affecting and belongs to the model specification.

---

## 85. Noncommuting Updates

Two updates:

`F_A`

and:

`F_B`

do not commute when:

`F_A(F_B(x)) ≠ F_B(F_A(x))`.

In that case their execution order must remain fixed for deterministic replay.

---

## 86. Simultaneous Evaluation

A synchronous scheme may evaluate several next-state quantities from the same previous state before committing them together.

The state visibility rules must be explicit.

---

## 87. Sequential Evaluation

A sequential scheme may expose updated intermediate state to later operations within the same numerical cycle.

This defines different semantics from synchronous evaluation.

---

## 88. Predictor-Corrector Evolution

A predictor-corrector method may estimate intermediate state and then refine it.

The predictor state is numerical state, not committed ternary state unless explicitly committed.

---

## 89. Implicit Coupled Solve

A model may solve continuous and feedback state implicitly.

This requires a defined convergence rule.

No implicit solve is assumed by the foundational TR numerical layer.

---

## 90. Iterative Solver State

If an iterative solver is used, its internal state may affect deterministic restart.

The restart contract must define whether internal iteration state is preserved.

---

## 91. Numerical Convergence

Convergence of an iterative numerical solver is distinct from asymptotic stability of the modeled dynamical system.

---

## 92. Convergence Tolerance

A numerical convergence tolerance:

`epsilon_solver`

belongs to the solver contract.

It does not apply to exact ternary-state equality.

---

## 93. Floating-Point Arithmetic

Floating-point realizations introduce finite-precision approximation in continuous quantities.

Categorical ternary state remains exact at the semantic layer.

---

## 94. Fixed-Point Arithmetic

A specialization may use fixed-point arithmetic for selected continuous or derived quantities.

Scaling, saturation, rounding, and overflow behavior must be explicit.

---

## 95. Fixed-Point Ternary State

Machine encoding of ternary state remains distinct from fixed-point encoding of continuous quantities.

---

## 96. Quantization

Numerical quantization of a continuous variable does not itself create ternary semantics.

The continuous-to-ternary mapping remains a separately defined classification.

---

## 97. Rounding

Rounding error may affect numerical threshold comparisons.

The comparison policy must be deterministic where deterministic replay is required.

---

## 98. Saturation

Saturation may bound a numerical representation.

It does not prove boundedness of the formal continuous model.

---

## 99. Overflow

Overflow is a numerical error or implementation condition.

It is not active neutral.

---

## 100. Underflow

Underflow is likewise a numerical representation effect.

It does not carry ternary semantics.

---

## 101. NaN

A non-finite value:

`NaN`

must use an explicit error or invalid-state path.

It must not silently map to:

`0`.

---

## 102. Infinity

Positive or negative infinity must likewise be handled explicitly unless included in the mathematical domain by definition.

---

## 103. Invalid Numerical State

An invalid numerical state belongs to an implementation-status space.

It remains distinct from:

`T = {-1, 0, 1}`.

---

## 104. Exact Categorical State

The ternary states:

`-1`

`0`

`1`

are categorical values.

Their equality is exact.

---

## 105. Numerical Approximation Boundary

Approximation applies upstream of classification and to continuous outputs.

It does not weaken categorical execution invariants.

---

## 106. Error Propagation

Numerical error may propagate through:

`continuous state`

`→ resonance state`

`→ decision variable`

`→ target boundary`.

A small continuous error near a decision boundary may alter the target classification.

---

## 107. Classification Sensitivity

Target sensitivity near a boundary should be distinguished from numerical instability of the continuous solver.

---

## 108. Decision Margin

A decision margin may quantify distance from a target boundary.

Large margin can reduce classification sensitivity to small numerical perturbations.

---

## 109. Resonance Margin

A resonance margin may similarly quantify distance from:

`∂W_R`.

It remains distinct from target margin unless the mapping identifies them.

---

## 110. Hysteresis Numerical Role

Hysteresis can reduce rapid target switching near noisy numerical boundaries.

Its exact thresholds remain model-specific.

---

## 111. Persistence Numerical Role

Persistence can require repeated boundary satisfaction before changing target.

The persistence counter becomes numerical state.

---

## 112. Filtering Numerical Role

A filtered decision variable may suppress high-frequency numerical fluctuation.

The filter state belongs to restart state.

---

## 113. Event Chatter

Repeated event detection around one boundary may occur due to finite precision or dynamics.

The event-handling policy must define:

- hysteresis;
- deadband;
- minimum step;
- event lockout

where used.

---

## 114. Event Lockout

A temporary lockout may prevent immediate repeated triggering of the same event.

This is event-control state.

It is not active neutral.

---

## 115. Numerical Deadband

A numerical deadband is a finite decision region.

It is not identical to ternary state:

`0`.

A mapping may choose to map it to:

`0`, but that relation must be explicit.

---

## 116. Event Queue

A hybrid numerical implementation may maintain an event queue.

If event ordering affects future state, the queue belongs to complete numerical state.

---

## 117. Event Priority

Simultaneous events require a priority or joint-transition rule.

The ordering must be deterministic when deterministic replay is required.

---

## 118. Event Timestamp

Each event may carry:

- physical time;
- numerical step;
- execution tact.

The required timestamp set depends on the trace contract.

---

## 119. Event Ordering at Equal Time

Two events may share the same physical timestamp while remaining ordered within the computational update.

The ordering must be represented if result-affecting.

---

## 120. Target Event versus Commit Event

A target-change event and committed ternary transition remain separate.

Their timestamps may coincide or differ.

---

## 121. First-Leg Event versus Second-Leg Event

The two route legs remain separately timestamped events.

---

## 122. Numerical Subcycling

A continuous subsystem may perform:

`N_sub`

integration steps per execution tact.

---

## 123. Subcycle Mapping

If:

`n`

is numerical step and:

`k`

execution tact, a fixed subcycling rule may use:

`n = k N_sub + j`.

---

## 124. Variable Subcycling

Adaptive subcycling may use a variable number of numerical steps per execution tact.

The mapping becomes result-affecting state.

---

## 125. Scheduler Supercycle

A scheduler may operate over multiple execution tacts.

The cycle position must be retained when it affects execution eligibility.

---

## 126. Multirate Evolution

Different subsystems may use different rates:

- phase update rate;
- frequency update rate;
- resonance evaluation rate;
- target rate;
- scheduler rate;
- routing rate;
- feedback rate.

---

## 127. Synchronization Point

A synchronization point is a coordinate at which selected subsystem states are mutually visible for coupling.

The visibility rule must be explicit.

---

## 128. Stale-State Access

A subsystem may intentionally consume a previously sampled state.

This is delayed access at the numerical interface.

It must not be mislabeled as explicit pairwise physical phase delay unless that physical delay is actually modeled.

---

## 129. Numerical Latency

Computational staging may introduce one or more numerical-step latencies.

These are implementation or numerical latencies.

---

## 130. Numerical Latency versus Physical Delay

The distinction remains:

`numerical latency ≠ physical temporal delay`

unless the model explicitly equates them through a timing contract.

---

## 131. Phase Lag versus Numerical Latency

Likewise:

`phase lag ≠ numerical latency`.

---

## 132. Scheduler Latency

A target may wait until a scheduler commit opportunity.

This waiting time is execution latency.

---

## 133. Route Latency

An opposite target requires two state-changing commits and may include neutral residence.

Its total route latency depends on scheduler and route policy.

---

## 134. Feedback Latency

Feedback may be applied immediately after commit or at a later coupling boundary.

The implementation must define this timing.

---

## 135. Deterministic Numerical Evolution

A deterministic numerical realization must produce the same next state for identical:

- complete current state;
- parameters;
- inputs;
- update order;
- arithmetic semantics.

---

## 136. Deterministic Replay

A deterministic replay reproduces the same trajectory or declared canonical artifacts from an identical complete checkpoint.

---

## 137. Replay State

A replay-complete state may include:

- continuous state;
- phase;
- retained frequency;
- resonance state;
- target;
- executed state;
- pending state;
- scheduler state;
- route state;
- counters;
- queue state;
- adaptive thresholds;
- filter state;
- solver state;
- random state where applicable.

---

## 138. Replay Parameters

Immutable or externally supplied parameters must also be preserved.

Examples include:

- timestep;
- coupling coefficients;
- phase-lag parameters;
- target thresholds;
- scheduler configuration;
- capacity limits.

---

## 139. Replay Input Stream

Future external inputs must be identical for deterministic replay.

---

## 140. Canonical Ordering

Iteration order over entities, edges, queues, or events may affect finite-precision results.

A deterministic implementation should define canonical ordering when required.

---

## 141. Reduction Ordering

Floating-point reductions are not generally associative.

Therefore deterministic reduction order may be required for byte-identical replay.

---

## 142. Parallel Execution

Parallel evaluation may change reduction ordering.

A reproducibility contract must define whether exact or tolerance-based comparison is required.

---

## 143. Exact Replay

Exact replay may require:

- identical arithmetic;
- identical ordering;
- identical platform semantics;
- canonical serialization.

---

## 144. Numerical Replay

A weaker replay contract may compare continuous quantities within tolerance while requiring categorical state equality exactly.

The comparison policy must be explicit.

---

## 145. Mixed Replay Semantics

A hybrid replay may use:

- exact equality for ternary states;
- exact equality for categorical scheduler state;
- tolerance for floating-point continuous quantities;
- canonical event ordering.

---

## 146. Random State

A stochastic numerical model must preserve random state for reproducibility.

The seed alone may be insufficient if generator state has advanced.

---

## 147. Random Generator State

Where stochastic evolution is used, restart-complete state may include full generator state.

---

## 148. Deterministic Ternary Layer under Stochastic Upstream Dynamics

Even when continuous inputs are stochastic, the ternary execution mapping can remain deterministic conditional on the realized upstream state and control state.

---

## 149. Numerical Error

Let:

`e_n`

denote numerical approximation error.

The exact definition depends on the reference solution and norm.
